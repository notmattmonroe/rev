Title: Your diet will crush me
Date: 2007-03-14 20:20
Slug: your-diet-will-crush-me
Category: personal
Status: published

I've finally bitten the bullet and tried once again to use an <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_aggregator">RSS aggregator</a> to simplify my life.  It might work this time.  I've opted for <a href="http://www.google.com/reader/view/">Google Reade</a>r, because it integrates nicely into <a href="http://www.google.com/ig">Google Homepage</a>.  So whenever I hit the home button I've got all my blog/news update along with <a href="http://www.gmail.com/">gmail</a> and <a href="http://www.google.com/calendar">calendar</a>.  It's the digital dashboard that tech visionaries have been promising since the mid 90's.   For my regular daily reading, easily 80% is only one click from my browser default page.  Instead of opening 15 different sites in tabs in the morning to see which have updated with what I simply look at the list of items and plow through them much quicker.  It's all about simplification.<br /><br /> The only media feed I have is <a href="http://news.bbc.co.uk/">BBC</a>.  I was trying to figure out why I made the decision to only include area specific sites (<a href="http://www.macworld.com/">macworld</a>, <a href="http://idolator.com/">idolator</a>, <a href="http://stlbloggers.com/">blogs</a>, <a href="http://reddit.com/">reddit</a>) with no general daily news source.  Listening to <a href="http://www.theskepticsguide.org/">the Skeptics Guide</a> podcast on the way to work it clicked.  <br /><br />Daily news media makes you stupider.  For local news, sports,  and general politics and business news (read: press releases) they're fine.   For anything that require the slightest bit of nuance or complexity you are going to be dumber about a topic after reading about it in the newspaper or watching it on the evening news.<br /><br /> The story that brought this to clarity was about a recent study <a href="http://jama.ama-assn.org/cgi/content/abstract/297/9/969">comparing four popular diets</a>.  The media has concluded that the Atkins diet is the best of all possible diets (again).  <a href="http://med.stanford.edu/profiles/Christopher_Gardner/">Christopher Gardner</a>, the lead author,  was on <a href="http://sciencefriday.com/">Science Friday</a> and his first statement was that the media had blown the report all out of proportion to the actual results.  He said this was too short term a study to draw any significant conclusions about any particular diet.  The more important point to take away was probably that ALL of the diets were hard to follow in every day life.  He also noted that for the people who followed the diets most closely, there was no significant difference in the results regardless of which diet they were on.  Finally he said that there needs to be more research into long term impact of diet plans, as there is much criticism of all diets from the medical community.  He'd like to do a study where rather than pick a fad diet, they put people on one of three configurations of diets where the study provides them with high quality proteins, fats, and carbs in various balances to see the impact when quality and quantity of foods are controlled for.  He did also point out that for all it's other problems, Atkins' greatest strength is that it prohibits refined sugars which is most likely the main reason it is effective in short term weight loss.<br /><br /> This is one of the less egregious examples I've encountered, it's just the most recent.  I have often read a news article in a newspaper or on a website and then went to read the research the article was based on only to find that the research comes to the exact opposite conclusion of the newspaper article.<br /><br /> I suppose now I need to get off my lazy butt and make the comments on my blog available through rss.<br /><br />

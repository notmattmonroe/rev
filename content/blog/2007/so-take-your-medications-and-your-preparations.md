So thanks to celebrated research biologist Jenny McCarthy* the 'MMR vaccine causes autism' meme is out in the public once again.  I read a few summaries and figured her appearance was the normal anti-science nonsense that Oprah is so fond of but having read the transcript I have to say McCarthy is completely unbelievable.  Some of her statements are just flat out lies.  She claims that vaccines contain &quot;aborted fetal tissue&quot;.  That's possibly the most insane thing I've ever heard from the reliably illogical and unbalanced anti-vaccine viewpoint.  She further claims that when she brought her child in for the shot she told the doctor she had a bad feeling about it, and that the doctor laughed, insulted her, swore at her, and had his nurse forcibly vaccinate her son against her protests.  This is clearly a lie, otherwise he would be in jail.  Most of the doctors that I've read comments from on this are much more charitable and use phrases like &quot;memory becomes unreliable over time as emotional significance of an perceived event colors the accuracy of the reflection&quot; or &quot;a fantasy prone personality describing the scene as though it were part of a movie&quot;.  Let's call it what it is: a lie.  If I were her doctor I'd be suing for slander. <br /><br />The world of conspiracy theorists is a fairly dense and diverse one.  The particular subset of the autism related theorists comprise two overlapping groups: the generalized anti-vaccine crowd and the new age woo practicioners.  McCarthy seems to fall into the latter judging by all of the 'therapies' she's chosen and her statements on things like &quot;Indigo Children&quot; and other such superstition.  Both groups buy wholeheartedly into the theory that big drug companies are intentionally causing children to become autistic  by using Thimerosal in vaccines.  The inital study that triggered this entire industry, the Wakefield** one, has been so thoroughly debunked that even many of the anti-vaccine crowd try to sweep it under the rug.  I find it interesting that these people are so obsessed with their precious idea of a medical industry conspiracy that they don't stop for a moment to question the facts being sold to them by a couple of lawyers looking to drum up a big settlement from vaccine manufacturers.  Instead they cling to the idea that the entirety of the worldwide medical, scientific, and insurance industries in collusion with every single government in the world are conspiring to hide the link between vaccines and autism.   <br /><br />Let's consider that the link between Thimerosal was non-existent to begin with but the manufacturer's completely removed it from the vaccine anyway to calm the fears of parents.  And autism continued to rise.  And the theorists simply throw away their &quot;it's 100% absolutely definitely caused by the Thimerosal&quot; argument as though they'd never made it and say it's something else in the vaccine.  In the UK, where vaccination as a whole dropped in the late 90's as a result of the P.R. campaign by the anti-vaccine crowd autism has continued to rise, the opposite of the expected effect if vaccine has any impact.  Large scale studies in Sweden, Norway, and Japan (of hundreds of thousands of children, as opposed to Wakefields 12 cases) have shown no correlation whatsover between vaccination and autism.  <br /><br />Note also that of the hundreds of thousands of pediatricians just in the western hemisphere who are a part of the claimed conspiracy, a large percentage of them have children and give them the MMR shot.  If they knew it caused autism (or more accurately increased the likelihood of autism) why would they put their children at risk.  To say nothing of the millions of scientists, HMO managers, accountants, lab technicians, insurance agency investigators, lawyers, file clerks, etc etc.  The viability of a project to keep information from the public decreases exponentially over time and as the number of people involved increases.  A hundred people for a year or two?  Possibly, if they are highly motivated enough (think Manhattan Project).  Longer if they are in a tightly controlled information environment (think Soviet Union, U.S. Army medical experiments on black soldiers).  But a conspiracy involving literally millions of people over decades just isn't plausible.  The first person who revealed (and was able to prove, that being the key) the conspiracy would become an internationl hero and would go down in history as one of the most important people of the 21st century. <br /><br />As with most Fantasy Based Therapy (otherwise known as 'Alternative') my criticism is twofold:  In the first instance people are spending a lot of time and energy on 'treatments' and 'therapies' that DO NOT WORK ( e.g. homeopathy, accupuncture).  In the second instance many of these approaches can cause actual harm directly or through delaying actual medical treatment (e.g. herbs, chiropractic, chelation, psychic surgery, faith healing, etc etc). <br /><br />And of course Oprah provided a whole 20 seconds to read a comment from the CDC to the effect that &quot;we don't know what causes autism&quot;.  Wow, how 'fair and balanced' to provide uncritical coverage for 59 minutes and 40 seconds and provide an entire third of a minute for a fact based response.  And while they try to make much of the lack of absoluteness of the CDC response, the official word from the CDC is &quot;The weight of currently available scientific evidence does not support the hypothesis that vaccines cause autism.&quot; http://www.cdc.gov/od/science/iso/mmr_autism.htm  That seems pretty definite to me.  Sure, real scientists don't use emotionally loaded (read: manipulative) language.  They present the facts as they are.  Of course the real purpose of the show isn't to provide information, but rather to sell ads.  And touchy feely lies get better ratings.  To be clear, I believe that Oprah doesn't care one bit if the things she promotes on her show are true or not, as long as she keeps making money. <br /><br /><br /><br />*before anyone goes all &quot;ad hominem&quot; on me note this: Just because an argument is ad hominem does not make it invalid.  Saying she has a lot of loopy beliefs speaks directly to credibility, of which she has none. <br /><br />** to summarize Wakefield: He didn't disclose that he was being funded by lawyers hoping to sue the manufacturers, the test cases he used in the study were also litigants in the case, and he was applying for a patent on a vaccine that would compete directly with the MMR vaccine.
